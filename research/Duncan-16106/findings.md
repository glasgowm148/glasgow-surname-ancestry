# Findings: [Agnes Duncan, wife of James Glasgow](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Duncan-16106)

## Current conclusion

Reviewed 18 October 2026. **Agnes Duncan, wife of James Glasgow the weaver, was buried in Greyfriars on 21 January 1674. This is a burial date, not an exact death date; her indexed 1653 marriage and the family’s later identity questions remain separate.**



## Edinburgh apprentice brothers and the 1674 burial — 18 October 2026

James Glasgow-4114, apprenticed to William Adam, apothecary, on 22 April 1696, and Thomas Glasgow-3285, assigned to the baxter apprenticeship of 11 October 1699, are selected as probable brothers at 79% subjective confidence. Both entries name their father as James Glasgow, weaver and burgess of Edinburgh. The matching personal name, craft and civic status, in the same register only three years apart, favour one father. Thomas is expressly the youngest son. His 13 November 1684 baptism fits entry at almost fifteen, while the Duffe household has sons James baptised in 1679 and 1680, fitting the 1696 entry at about seventeen or sixteen. This is a coherent sibling group rather than a name-only regional comparison.

The 79% assesses the profile-to-profile sibling inference, not a literal brother clause or measured frequency. The apprentice entries do not name a mother, address or the father’s admission date; two matching craftsmen remain possible. Thomas’s apprenticeship-to-baptism identity is a maintained assignment, supported by age and locality rather than a repeated birth date. Do not choose between the 1679 and 1680 James children, merge either with James-4114, or transfer Isobell Duffe as his mother. The 1690 Lindsayland apprentice James-4131 remains a compatible comparison, including the possibility of a cancelled first apprenticeship. If that identity is established, its deceased father must be reconciled with the master appearing in 1693.

The printed Greyfriars register records Agnes Duncan, wife of James Glasgow, weaver, buried on 21 January 1674 (printed pp.196 and 252; PDF pp.212 and 268, visually checked). This fits the wife married in 1653 and removes a supposed overlapping-wife obstacle to identifying the older weaver James-1150 with the Duffe husband James-2682, whose known children begin in 1677. Different wives alone do not exclude successive marriages. The same surname page records an unnamed stillborn child of James the weaver on 16 January 1687 and another unnamed child on 18 March 1690. Neither is assigned to a named baptismal child or mother.

James-1150 and James-2682 remain compatible accounts of possibly one adult. James-2682’s circa-1655 birth is an estimate from his first known child, not an age record excluding the older man. The older craftsman’s long career and the late Duffe family are compatible, though fatherhood continuing to 1691 would be late. No marriage, wife clause or address ties the Duffe household directly to the 1649 burgess. Retain both accounts, their recorded families and present parent selections while testing that identity. James-1150’s proposed fatherhood of James-4114 stays probable 76%; James-2682’s Johnne-1151 placement stays speculative 26%, conditional on a distinct younger identity. These are different propositions, not independent corroboration. No mother, parent replacement, vital field or identity merger is selected.

The useful next discriminator is a weavers’ incorporation, testament or baptismal original giving the Duffe husband’s trade, address or prior marriage. A further same-name hit without such detail will not distinguish the adult accounts. The 1701–1760 burgess PDF at Trades House starts with Hepburn and cannot establish absence under Glasgow. The maintained 30 September ScotsFind transcript review covers that missing surname section, but the original Part I page remains uninspected.
