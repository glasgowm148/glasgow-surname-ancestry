== Biography ==

=== Alexander’s life in its local setting ===

Alexander’s records describe a family rooted in a small part of County Antrim, with children whose later lives lead beyond it. His clearest surviving household is at Lisnagaver in 1851: an Antrim-born farmer, his wife Mary, and three children. Earlier baptisms recover the beginnings of that family; later marriages and the Elder tenancy connect it to the next generation. The probable Gortahar death completes the broad outline, with the last identity link still open.



==== The places in his life ====


Gortereghy<ref name="Evidencec9d92bcd01">[https://www.townlands.ie/en/antrim/kilconway/rasharkin/killoquin-upper/gortereghy/ Gortereghy].</ref>, written Gortereagh in the baptism transcription, adjoins Lisnagaver<ref name="Evidence7d988f87c8">[https://www.townlands.ie/en/antrim/kilconway/rasharkin/killoquin/lisnagaver/ Lisnagaver].</ref> to the south. Gortahar<ref name="Evidence10a9c7be48">[https://www.townlands.ie/antrim/kilconway/rasharkin/killoquin/gortahar/ Gortahar].</ref> lies northeast, beyond Moneyleck. They are distinct townlands within the same parish. The map shows approximate modern townland locations, not reconstructed houses or field boundaries.


Both spouses reported first marriage in 1836. Their children’s baptisms place them at Gortereagh in 1837–38 and Lisnagaver by 1843. That suggests a change in the family’s recorded residence between neighbouring townlands; it does not date a move precisely or establish a return to a paternal home. Mary’s possible local family connections deserve investigation in their own right.


The census’s farmer and literate household, the small assessed tenancy and the children’s marriages recover part of Alexander’s circumstances. Later records call him a labourer, and his son-in-law succeeds him on the holding. These changes are compatible with an altered working role as he aged, but their cause is unknown. No inspected source establishes eviction, poverty, or illness-induced retirement. The record sequence and farm analysis distinguish those observations from assumptions about his life.


The following account traces marriage and children, examines the surviving household, then follows the farm, probable death and paternal connections. Missing parentage is one question within that family history.



=== Marriage, Mary’s family and the children ===

Alexander Glasgow and Mary/Molly McCaughan are a documented couple. Their 1851 household reports first marriage in 1836, but no original marriage entry has been located. Rasharkin Presbyterian’s surviving marriage register begins in 1845; First Kilraughts material (Coleraine Family History Society film 197, marriages from 1831) remains a focused alternative.


The marriage-year statement fits the first directly located record sequence. The published transcription of the original Rasharkin Presbyterian register, PRONI MIC1P/292, records the same parental couple at Gortereagh: James Glascaw was born 17 January and baptised 9 February 1837 to Alex and Mary McAughin; an incomplete 1838 entry records a Glascow child of Alex and Molly McAughern; and a later James Glascow was born 9 October 1843 and baptised 7 January 1844 to Alex and Molly McAughern at Lisnagaver. The named transcription can be checked at Rasharkin Presbyterian baptisms<ref name="Evidence5574dbd555">[https://genealogy.torrens.org/BannValley/church/RasharkinP/Baptisms_1834.html Rasharkin Presbyterian baptisms].</ref>. The register transcription preserves Mary/Molly and McAughin/McAughern. The repeated parental names and residences support their identification with Mary McCaughan while retaining the spellings actually recorded.


The Gortereagh residence is meaningful maternal context, but it is not a parentage proof. The 1834 tithe list includes William and James McCaheran there, a spelling compatible with the recorded McAughin/McAughern/McCaughan forms. This makes a McCaughan family connection a reasonable explanation to test for the newly married couple’s residence, perhaps through Mary's people or a subtenancy. It does not prove that either man was Mary's father, nor that the first son James was named for a maternal grandfather. The 1851 Lisnagaver evidence supplies a more concrete lead: Mary McCaheren, 22, lived with farmer Archibald McCaheren, 60, and Elizabeth in house 31. She is the strongest currently located candidate for the later 1855 bride who named her father as Archy, but that is a separate McCaughan problem. It does not establish Mary Glasgow’s parentage. Likewise, the household of widowed Sarah McCaghrin and grandchildren beside widowed Margaret Elder and children is evidence of a local network of families and neighbours, not an expressed kinship.


The Gortereagh names are recorded in the 1834 Rasharkin tithe book, PRONI '''FIN/5/A/236'''; the 1851 McCaheren and Elder groupings are in Lisnagaver houses 31 and 5. These are separate record observations, not confirmed maternal relatives. See the National Archives census collection<ref name="Evidence0b09d5bb14">[https://nationalarchives.ie/collections/search-the-census/ National Archives census collection].</ref> and PRONI tithe-book guide<ref name="Evidenceb39783b2e9">[https://www.nidirect.gov.uk/articles/tithe-applotment-books PRONI tithe-book guide].</ref>.

==== What the gaps mean ====


The early children are documented separately from their inferred deaths. The table’s reuse-of-name argument does not supply an exact death date. Nor does the incomplete 1838 transcription identify its child as Samuel: an original copy of '''MIC1P/292, entry 106''' is needed to read the missing name and dates.


The transcription has no entries for 1840 or 1841, around Robert’s likely birth. Failure to find his baptism in that transcription is therefore weak evidence against the known parentage: the census and marriage positively establish his father. The missing original marriage, incomplete baptism entry and missing burial records are different gaps and need different searches.


The unnamed child would have been about twelve or thirteen in 1851. No resident, absent-person or household-death entry records that child in the maintained return audit. That omission weighs against an uncomplicated survival-and-emigration reconstruction, but cannot fix a death date or guarantee that the return was complete. The candidate Samuel Glasgow in Michigan was rejected because no American record supplied the required Antrim/parent bridge and the proposal also had to explain this omission. An impossible February date in a later cemetery transcription cannot name the child. The [[Glasgow-3940|child’s assessment]] preserves the comparison.

=== Following the children into adult life ===


==== Robert: the recorded son and the Scottish continuation ====


Robert, aged ten in 1851, is directly recorded as Alexander and Mary’s son in the same Lisnagaver household. His 5 April 1866 marriage to Jane Graham<ref name="Evidence05c8c53b0b">[https://civilrecords.irishgenealogy.ie/churchrecords/images/marriage_returns/marriages_1866/11531/8240963.pdf 5 April 1866 marriage to Jane Graham].</ref> independently calls him a Lisnagaver farmer and names his father Alexander Glasgow, farmer. Those two records make Robert Glasgow-938 the documented child, with estimated birth about 1840–41 from the census and marriage ages. The often repeated exact 6 November 1840 birth date has no identified supporting original. His probable identification with a later Gortereghy valuation occupier is useful local continuity, but that identification is separate from the proved father–son relationship.


Robert’s later family is followed into Glasgow, Scotland, in the maintained account, which cites a 1871 Scottish census index for Robert<ref name="Evidencea991c3602f">[https://www.familysearch.org/ark:/61903/1:1:QV9Y-71S6 1871 Scottish census index for Robert].</ref> and an 1881 index for the younger Alexander<ref name="Evidencecfab144f27">[https://www.familysearch.org/ark:/61903/1:1:VQQ8-5MB 1881 index for the younger Alexander].</ref>. [[Glasgow-937|Alexander Glasgow (Glasgow-937)]], born about 1871, is the next historical generation in that family reconstruction. The Irish census and marriage provide the direct father–son argument for Alexander-951 and Robert; the Scottish continuation relies here on the maintained census references, not newly inspected original images. The copied exact birth date for the younger Alexander had been cited to the same index used for the census, so it is not adopted as an independently verified birth in this account. Exact Scottish address chronology and Robert’s reported 1902 death remain checks on their own originals.


This carries the story beyond the Antrim farm without making every copied detail equally secure. The tested descendant’s confirmed paternal ancestry reaches Alexander-951; the Y-DNA section explains the genetic comparison without listing living generations.


The precise Scottish birth-image leads and the misplaced 1902 death citation are assessed in the next research tests. Neither has yet been inspected; the direct Irish father–son evidence remains secure.



==== James: a strong Michigan candidate, still an open identity ====


The second James, Glasgow-973, has an unusually clear Irish identity: the 1843–44 baptism and his age seven appearance in the 1851 household establish him as Alexander and Mary’s child. His subsequent association with the James Glasgow buried at Tecumseh, Michigan is strong but still unproved. The Michigan household is well reconstructed: the original 1884 Michigan census image<ref name="Evidence13cf3f6b80">[https://michigan.access.preservica.com/uncategorized/IO_eb4d3f94-3c2f-4701-b5bb-c3ecee9ef9b9/ the original 1884 Michigan census image].</ref> gives a forty-year-old Irish-born baggage agent, and the Brookside register identifies the associated wife and daughters. His daughter Minnie’s 1941 death certificate<ref name="Evidence8b5b1f0905">[https://michigan.access.preservica.com/uncategorized/IO_3dab599e-1093-473c-be85-eee5e9b49154/ 1941 death certificate].</ref> reports James as born in County Antrim. That narrows the comparison materially but names neither Alexander nor Mary and gives no Lisnagaver detail. The 1869 original marriage return, naturalisation papers, or a passenger record is still required before the Michigan life becomes Alexander’s son’s biography.



==== Ann: a later marriage and the Nancy problem ====


Ann, Glasgow-438, is likewise direct family evidence. She was aged four in 1851 and her 13 October 1876 marriage to Robert McCracken<ref name="Evidence157ee58a9a">[https://www.irishgenealogy.ie/files/civil/marriage_returns/marriages_1876/11181/8097170.pdf 13 October 1876 marriage to Robert McCracken].</ref> calls her a full-age spinster of Lisnagaver and names Alexander Glasgow, labourer, as father. The occupational wording matters because it independently makes the 1888 Gortahar death’s “labourer” compatible with the former Lisnagaver farmer; it is not grounds to split the two men. Ann’s later married surname also means Nancy Glasgow, who reported Alexander’s 1888 death, cannot simply be equated with her. A separate daughter, or another explanation, remains possible.



==== Mary: the daughter whose records connect the households ====


Mary Glasgow-3167 is not in the 1851 return but is securely added by her 14 June 1871 marriage to Robert Elder<ref name="Evidence5782477e56">[https://www.irishgenealogy.ie/files/civil/marriage_returns/marriages_1871/11341/8162804.pdf 14 June 1871 marriage to Robert Elder].</ref>: aged 28, resident Lisnagaver, she named Alexander Glasgow, farmer, as father. Her marriage, Robert Elder’s succession to lot 25, and the Gortahar deaths create the high-confidence bridge between the Lisnagaver couple and Mary McCaughan’s later identity. Mary Glasgow’s 17 February 1892 death return<ref name="Evidence6f1ed437a9">[https://www.irishgenealogy.ie/files/civil/deaths_returns/deaths_1892/06049/4723532.pdf 17 February 1892 death return].</ref> records daughter Mary Elder as informant. It does not state a maiden surname or husband, and its reported age 89 conflicts with the retained c.1811 estimate; those limits belong beside the identification, not beneath it.


Mary’s later household also contains an instructive name conflict. Her granddaughter’s 1908 birth<ref name="Evidencebe365902e1">[https://www.irishgenealogy.ie/files/civil/birth_returns/births_1908/01647/1657700.pdf 1908 birth].</ref> records '''Samuel Elder''', son of Jane Elder, with no father named; the 1911 household<ref name="Evidenceb4f7bd3ba5">[https://nationalarchives.ie/collections/search-the-census/census-record/#id=490671&c20_year=1911 1911 household].</ref> calls him '''Samuel Glasgow'''. The surname is genuinely present in the census, not merely a modern transcription mistake. These records help reconstruct the continuing family, but neither identifies his father. It would be circular to invent a Glasgow father and then use that invented relationship to strengthen Alexander’s ancestry.


Mary’s absence in 1851 is consequently an unresolved location question within a well-supported later family identification. It does not erase the 1871 father statement. Nor does it establish that she was the Maryan in John of Glenleslie’s household: the roughly nine-year conflict between their implied birth years requires explanation, and no located record joins those two identities.

==== The reported 1829 occurrence: an age and identity test ====


The original newspaper alert of '''12 September 2025''' was inspected directly on 13 September 2026. Its MyHeritage result, 12021-100828509<ref name="Evidenceb9eaffcccc">[https://www.myheritage.com/research/record-12021-100828509-/alexander-glasgow-in-united-kingdom-names-stories-in-newspapers-from-oldnewscom MyHeritage result, 12021-100828509].</ref>, does quote '''“Alex. Glasgow, Lisnagaver”''' from the '''Belfast News-Letter, 5 June 1829'''. The British Newspaper Archive issue index<ref name="Evidence2721035739" /> independently locates the Lisnagaver entry in '''page 4, article 015'''. Its OCR mangles Glasgow, explaining why a surname search missed the article while a townland search found it. These are two derivative extracts of one newspaper occurrence, not two independent historical records.


The archive’s damaged heading excerpt appears to concern persons applying to register, with special sessions at Ballymoney, under '''County of Antrim'''. That makes a registration notice the specific working context, but the full image and governing columns remain unread: MyHeritage now presents a subscription paywall and the archive viewer requires sign-in. The extract cannot yet establish an adult qualification, successful registration or the man’s identity. The earlier claim that even the alert was uninspected is superseded; the historical interpretation remains open.

The important distinction is between '''an older birth date for the same man''' and '''a second, older man'''. Even if the notice proves adult status, it would not automatically make the subject Alexander's father. Conversely, identifying the subject from the 1888 age and then using him to validate that same death identification would be circular. No birth estimate or parent ranking changes on the extract alone.


If the subject is Alexander-951, the occurrence would support a premarital Lisnagaver association before the couple's Gortereagh baptisms of 1837–38 and their documented Lisnagaver residence by 1843. That would make a temporary residence near Mary's possible family a more focused hypothesis; it would not prove a move, tenancy or inheritance.


The supplied extract also lists Robert Elder of '''Gortaragh''', Arthur O'Neil of Tehorney, Henry and James Wallace of Culmore, James Tomb of Ballymaconly and James Elder of Drumcon. Their combined names and literal place spellings are useful for locating the correct column despite imperfect OCR. They must not yet be treated as neighbours, fellow freeholders or relatives. The 1829 Robert Elder cannot comfortably be the later son-in-law whose 1871 marriage<ref name="Evidence5782477e56" /> gives age forty and father Nathaniel; the corresponding 1894 death<ref name="Evidencea1cad7ff9e">[https://www.irishgenealogy.ie/files/civil/deaths_returns/deaths_1894/05952/4691448.pdf 1894 death].</ref> gives age sixty-four. Those dates place that Robert's birth around 1830–31. A different Elder generation is therefore the appropriate comparison, and '''Gortaragh''' is left unnormalised pending the page.


The next test is the full image of page 4 and the heading governing this entry, with adjoining columns. The earlier PRONI freeholder-search negative does not dispose of this newspaper occurrence. The surname-search failure is now demonstrably an OCR limitation: the exact townland retrieves article 015. Neither search outcomes nor damaged heading text determine the qualification or age of the named man.



==== What the land records can establish ====


Alexander could have been the 1834 occupier at about twenty-three. An older namesake remains possible, but the name alone does not require one. In the 1836 field book, the house section names Robert Glasgow and Archibald McCaughan junior; the land section supplies no occupier names. Alexander’s omission cannot prove he held no land, lived with Robert, or had not yet formed a household.

=== The wider family: proved links and open bridges ===

The Lisnagaver evidence supports a local Glasgow network; it does not yet yield a single reconstructed pedigree. The starting point is the 1851 sequence. House 34<ref name="Evidence2c97cf113b" /> contains John Glasgow, farmer, with wife Margaret and his unmarried brother Samuel, a linen weaver. House 35<ref name="Evidencedea66e5c28" /> contains Alexander and Mary with their children. House 37<ref name="Evidence6c8cd27a9f" /> contains Thomas Glasgow and Mary Ann. A Watters household separates Alexander and Thomas. The layout establishes a small same-surname farming cluster and useful geographic proximity; enumeration order is not a kinship statement or a property map.


John Glasgow-867 and Samuel Glasgow-2908 belong to a specific '''father-John branch'''. John’s original 16 November 1846 marriage<ref name="Evidencef940c7acb4">[https://www.irishgenealogy.ie/view/?record_id=cima-3156063 original 16 November 1846 marriage].</ref> names his father as John Glasgow, farmer. The 1851 household expressly calls Samuel John’s brother. That chain directly establishes the brotherhood and names John-867’s father and his occupation. Extending that father statement to Samuel assumes they shared a father; the census does not distinguish full from half brothers. It does '''not''' identify that father with John Glasgow-1022, the older Glenleslie man, and it does not make Alexander another son. The younger John’s memorial says he died in 1895 aged 78, which implies a birth about 1816–17 and makes the candidate Glasgow-1022 less comfortable than an earlier census-age reading alone. Any maintained attachment to John-1022 is an uncertain identification of the father, not an additional record and not evidence for Alexander’s parentage.


The distinction matters because the neighbouring tenancy pattern is tempting but not decisive. The 1834 tithe names Alexander and Robert Glasgow at Lisnagaver, while later valuation lots 24, 25 and 26 are associated with John, Alexander and Thomas under Robert Harvey. Acreage, field-book and adjacency arguments can suggest tenancy succession or a broader network. They cannot establish a biological descent, and Plantation-measure tithe acreage cannot be numerically matched to statute-measure valuation acreage to manufacture an inheritance. The 1836 field book names Robert Glasgow and Archibald McCaughan junior in the house section but does not name Alexander; its land section does not supply occupier names. It therefore cannot decide who lived together, who inherited, or whether Alexander was a brother of John or Thomas.


Thomas Glasgow-987 and Daniel Glasgow-3896 have a stronger relationship argument than Alexander has with either. Daniel’s 1871 will, indexed through the PRONI Wills Calendar<ref name="Evidence968b317eb1">[https://apps.proni.gov.uk/willscalendar_ie/willssearch.aspx PRONI Wills Calendar].</ref>, calls the late Thomas his brother and identifies Daniel Neely Glasgow as nephew; Daniel Neely’s 28 June 1888 marriage<ref name="Evidence47f7d35ebe">[https://www.irishgenealogy.ie/files/civil/marriage_returns/marriages_1888/10763/5925382.pdf 28 June 1888 marriage].</ref> in turn names Thomas Glasgow, farmer, as his father. Those two records establish Daniel and Thomas as brothers. Thomas’s PRONI calendar administration<ref name="Evidence9c6dade023">[https://apps.proni.gov.uk/WillsCalendar_IE/WillsSearch.aspx PRONI calendar administration].</ref> adds that the Lisnagaver farmer died 21 June 1870 and administration went to widow Mary Anne on 7 September. It was an intestate administration, not a will, and it names no parent, sibling or heir beyond the widow. It cannot be used to extend the proved brotherhood into a parentage for Alexander.


Alexander may be a brother or cousin of Thomas and Daniel. Shared locality, associated land, church context and descendant testimony make that a worthwhile research model. No confirmed Y-DNA comparison with an independently documented Thomas or Daniel descendant is supplied in this case. None of those sources says that Alexander was their brother, so the three men are not a proved sibling set. The 2020 correspondence records the older Alexander-and-Rosanna belief, but provides no demonstrably independent parentage source: the correspondent said she ''believed'' Thomas’s parents were Alexander and Rosanna, separately wondered whether the user’s Alexander was Thomas’s brother, and supplied neither a record citation nor usable autosomal-match data. The later FamilySearch Pedigree Resource File entries derive from an Ancestry member tree and conflict even on Rosanna’s reported death day. They show the circulation of a tradition, not its verification.

| Descendant DNA || Tested genetic relationships, with the panel and result specified || Linking a result to a historical ancestor depends on the descent; mutation evidence cannot fill every documentary gap || Establishes the paternal cluster and tests competing branch arrangements

An index, a transcription and a tree quoting the same original do not provide three independent confirmations. The surname-project report, STR reconstruction and later discussion can likewise reuse the same genetic observations. Independent civil, church and land records are valuable because they offer different routes through the identity problem; even then, their logical dependencies must remain visible.



==== What an unsuccessful search means ====


Missing evidence is useful only when we know what should have been recorded and what survives. The unnamed 1838 child’s absence from all relevant parts of the household return has more bearing on a survival hypothesis than an unrestricted web search finding nothing. It still cannot establish a death date by itself. A gap in the baptism transcription, a register starting after the marriage, and an archive image requiring onsite access are three different limitations.


The research history records completed searches and the wider register of inaccessible originals and unresolved identities; the focused next tests appear below. The next work is prioritised by the proposition it could resolve, not by the number of databases left to search. No numerical parent score is treated as a probability or allowed to replace these source-based arguments.
