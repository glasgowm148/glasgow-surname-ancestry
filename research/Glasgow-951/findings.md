### Parent-record search pause and exhaustion audit, 26 August 2026

No concrete record identifying Alexander's father or mother was found. The
current attachment to Robert remains uncertain, and the Alexander/Rosanna
tradition still applies directly only to Thomas; no record yet proves that
Alexander and Thomas were brothers.

The following searches were completed without a parentage result:

#### Newly identified direct-parentage source, 19 August 2026

A fresh audit of the full [PRONI church guide](https://www.proni.gov.uk/files/proni/2026-01/PRONI%20Guide%20to%20Church%20Records.pdf)
and [live eCatalogue](https://apps.proni.gov.uk/eCatNI_IE/SearchPage.aspx) found a record
group missing from the earlier target list: **Ballymoney/Dervock/Kilraughts
Reformed Presbyterian register, `CR5/32/1/1/1-2`, 1804-1844**. The catalogue's
full description says it contains lists of members by society for Dervock and
Kilraughts, with baptisms, marriages, deaths and removals including emigration;
Kilraughts coverage runs to about 1831. An
[Ulster Historical Foundation account](https://ulsterhistoricalfoundation.com/presbyterianism/covenanters/records-of-congregations)
describes the same volume as containing almost annual congregational censuses.

This is now the single best direct-parentage source. It spans Alexander's circa
1811 birth and Rosanna's reported 23 October 1822 death, and its family-group
lists may show Alexander with a father even if no baptism survives. It could
also test whether clockmaker Daniel and Thomas belonged to the same parental
household. PRONI marks the original `CR5/32/1/1/1` **On-Site Only**. The open
archival copy `CR5/32/1/1/2` has no remote digital-record link either, so the
images could not be searched from here; a targeted PRONI lookup or visit is
required. A targeted request for every surname variant hit, its household or
society heading, and adjacent context was sent to PRONI on 19 August 2026 as a
reply to the existing enquiry. No paid work was authorised; an estimate was
requested first.

Nearby early Church of Ireland registers were also audited: Kilrea baptisms
begin in 1801 (`MIC1/54/2-3, 55`), Ahoghill and Finvoy in 1811, Loughguile in
1802, and Killaig Presbyterian in 1805. The online Kilrea transcription and
graveyard list contain no Glasgow, but both are explicitly partial and omit the
critical 1809-11 baptism span, so this is not a valid negative. The exact
Rosanna death date found in the 2007 descendant post produced no independent
online record.

The watchmaker-Daniel trail was checked against the live/local profiles before
use. It duplicates existing work: [Daniel Glasgow
(Glasgow-3896)](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Glasgow-3896) is already proved by

#### James's Michigan identity lead materially strengthened

Alexander's son [James Glasgow (Glasgow-973)](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Glasgow-973)
now has a strong, but still unproved, later-life candidate. The Brookside
Cemetery register at Tecumseh, Michigan, records James Glasgow **1843-1896**
with wife Mary Baird and their children. More importantly, the original 1941
Michigan death certificate of daughter Minnie J. Boland names her father as
**James Glasgow, born County Antrim, Ireland**. Alexander's proved son was born
at Lisnagaver, County Antrim, on 9 October 1843.

This is the best post-1851 candidate yet found, but it does not identify James's
parents or exact birthday. Keep the identity unproved until a marriage,
naturalisation, census, passenger record or original death entry
connects the Michigan man to Alexander/Mary, Lisnagaver or a known sibling.
Michigan GENDIS gives the death as **30 July 1896**, not the 29 August date in
the current profile note. A supposed Dromore valuation error has also been
resolved: the entry is real, but original PRONI revision books carry James
Glasgow on the same small house-and-garden holding at Dromore from 1863-79
through 1891-1901. That continuing local valuation identity is unlikely to be
the Tecumseh man. Full evidence and source images are in the focused
[Glasgow-973 handoff](../Glasgow-973/findings.md).

The original 1884 Michigan state census now makes this more than a cemetery
coincidence. It records James, 40, an Irish-born baggage agent, with wife Mary
and daughters Minnie, Maggie and Bertha: the complete family independently
found in the Brookside register. It still does not supply his parents or exact
birthplace, so it strengthens but does not prove the identification.

A newly located contemporary report in the *Michigan Messenger*, 1 August
1896, p. 1, confirms the official date: James disappeared on Wednesday and his
body was found in the Raisin River near Blood's bridge on Thursday, 30 July.
It identifies him as the long-serving Lake Shore depot baggage master and
describes distinctive injuries—a scar across a previously broken nose and one
or two missing fingers on his right hand. This is valuable identifying evidence
for other American records, but it still names no parent or Irish locality.
The report's age estimate of about 55 suggests about 1841 and is less exact
than the 1843 cemetery year and age 40 in 1884. See the
[saved clipping and assessment](../Glasgow-973/findings.md).

#### Michigan Samuel rejected as the 1838 child

The 1838 baptism was already represented by
[Unknown Glasgow (Glasgow-3940)](https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Glasgow-3940),
attached to Alexander and Mary. It was not a new discovery. The live profile
records death before 1841, evidently as an inference rather than from a death
record.

The inference is reasonable but not conclusive. The 1851 Lisnagaver household
lists Alexander, Mary, Robert, James and Ann, with no additional person marked
resident, absent, or dead since 1841. The National Archives explains that this
census recorded household members absent on census night and family members
who had died since 1841. Nearby returns demonstrate both categories, including
children away as servants or pupils. If the 1838 child belonged to this same
family and was still alive in 1851, a 12- or 13-year-old would ordinarily be
expected either in the household or its absent section; if the child died from
1841 onward, the mortality section should ordinarily record the death.

Samuel Glasgow of Franklin/Blissfield, Michigan, therefore should **not** be
identified with `Glasgow-3940`. His compatible age and Irish birth are only
surname-level coincidence, while the family census is contrary evidence and no
American record names County Antrim or Alexander and Mary. Samuel reported
about 37 years in the United States in 1894, implying immigration around 1857,
so the same-person theory requires the unexplained omission of a living
13-year-old from every section of Alexander's 1851 return. The Pleasant View
transcription's impossible `29 February 1838` date further prevents exact-date
matching. Retain the Michigan records only as a rejected-candidate audit; do
not attach Samuel to this family. Full reasoning is in the focused
[Glasgow-3940 handoff](../Glasgow-3940/findings.md).

Robert's father William may belong to the McCracken household at Carnfinton,

Alexander `Glasgow-951` is directly documented at Gortereagh in 1837-38 and at

Older Alexander `Glasgow-3904` and Rosanna remain the leading specific parent
hypothesis from descendant tradition, still unproved.

The best no-PRONI continuation is now to search **through the sister's
children**, not only under her unknown married name. The National Archives of
Ireland 1841/1851 census-search database has a dedicated `Maiden Surname of
Mother` field. A search for mother `Glasgow`, restricted first to Rasharkin,
Kilconway and adjoining Antrim/Londonderry localities, could expose pension
applicants whose mother was an otherwise missing Glasgow sister and recover the
claimed 1841/1851 household. The live results service timed out on 13 August,
so this search remains pending rather than negative.

Scottish statutory deaths from 1855 are the other high-value route. Women are
indexed under maiden and married surnames, and an original death registration
can name both parents, including the mother's maiden surname. Search women born
in Ireland about 1790-1822 under Glasgow as either surname, then prioritise
Antrim/Rasharkin-linked census households. This route could prove both older
Alexander and Rosanna in one record; the Irish bride audit could only test the
father.

The land pattern slightly weakens any simple claim that Alexander and Thomas
merely divided one father's Lisnagaver farm. In 1834 Alexander held about 29
acres and Robert about 35. The later John-plus-Alexander holdings total about
30 acres, while Thomas held about 33 acres. This is compatible with two older
tenant blocks descending separately, although tenancy transfers need not follow
blood relationships and it does not exclude brotherhood. John belongs to a
documented father-John branch, so the cluster was not one obvious nuclear family.

A new collateral route may identify Thomas's parents without first proving
Alexander's sibling edge. Daniel Neely Glasgow, Thomas's proved son, was a
labourer residing in New York when his twins were born in September 1889. An
Irish-born cemetery labourer who died as Daniel `Glascow` at Troy on 12 February
1931 is a strong same-person candidate. His death certificate 9941 could name
Thomas and Mary Ann; the identification remains unproved until an original US
record supplies matching family detail.

Thomas's reported emigrant son Alexander is now bounded to a likely birth in
about 1851-1863: he was absent from Thomas's 1851 household, was not in the
public Rasharkin baptism transcription through 1846, and no suitable civil
birth appears from registration's start in 1864. Exact-name, Ireland-born
Ancestry searches returned no matching Alexander Glasgow in the 1900, 1910,
1920 or 1930 US censuses. The official South African archive index is more
promising: a same-named adult was defendant in Cape Supreme Court proceedings
in 1893 and 1898. Follow-up identifies both matters as Cape Town
commercial/property disputes: Gerhard Myburgh's executors brought the 1893
motion and mortgage holder Maria Lacey Welch brought the 1898 recovery action.
His identity and origin remain unproved.

367-pair father-son study observed 17 mutations and estimated a rate of about
`46.3 x 10^-3` per meiosis. Its shared `19` state is therefore a useful
secondary clue but particularly vulnerable to recurrence or back-mutation; it
must not be treated as a clade-defining event. [Mutation-rate study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12218846/)

The downloaded raw bundle resolves the private block more precisely. Kit
`B580327` has five exported private variants: `12648194 A>G`, `14337402 T>C`,
`14339231 G>C`, `15066888 A>T` and `15973436 A>C`. Its VCF also has a sixth
lineage-specific PASS call at `26534797 C>G` (depth 25). The non-matching screen
labels that position `FT124483`, but FT124483 is the different allele `C>A` at
the same multi-allelic site. It does **not** define a shared branch among the
other matches. All six calls are PASS at depth 25-78 and are absent from every
displayed match.

The bundle also permits direction to be assigned for the numeric match-side
differences: all 15 lie inside the B580327 quality BED and have no derived call
in its VCF. Most usefully, both `959947` and `200475` list `FT6135 C>T` at
`19389846`, while B580327 has a direct PASS ancestral call there (`GT 0/0`,
34 reference-C reads, one T read, one A read, depth 40) inside the quality BED.
At `FT20271` itself B580327 has a direct PASS derived call (`GT 1/1`, 57
alternate-T reads, depth 60), also inside the quality BED. That shared
off-tree state reinforces their common `DYS19=15`, `DYS456=16`, `DYS712=19`
pattern. Because FTDNA still leaves both kits terminal at FT20271, FT6135 is a
candidate pairing signal, not yet a confirmed child branch.

The three possible FT6135 outcomes are now explicit. Direct derived-T calls in
both James M. kits would place FT6135 at or above Robert `Glasgow-12` and show
that the pairwise omission was only a display/export limitation. Direct
ancestral-C calls in both would prevent that placement and require recurrence,
reversion, or a pedigree/tester-mapping review. No-call or inadequate coverage
would leave the marker unresolved. “Not displayed” is therefore not treated as
ancestral.

The FTE32242 lines are downstream of FT20271 rather than merely similar STR
matches, so they form a genuine paternal cousin branch. On the supplied
pedigrees the best present genealogical label for that branch is **descendants
of James M. Glasgow (`Glasgow-193`)**. The mutation could have arisen in the
transmission from Robert-12 to James-193 or in James's own paternal line; the
available data do not distinguish those biological formulations. Kit `254947`
remains a marker-only candidate. The common FT20271 ancestor is older
than Alexander and Robert-12, but the public results do not say how much older.
Until one of the other testers' paternal pedigrees is independently audited to
the branching generation, describe these men as **paternal genetic relatives**
or **genetic cousin lineages**, not numbered cousins.

Kit `254947` should be kept one evidential tier lower: its ancestor label closely
matches Robert `Glasgow-1143`, but the connection is based on the older Y-STR
cluster rather than a confirmed shared downstream SNP.

James `Glasgow-1025` is another possible collateral, not a located ancestor.
Kit `200475` shares Alexander's basal R-FT20271 branch and corresponds to the
Robert `Glasgow-12` line, despite the supplied result shortening the death year
to 1836 and reporting Scotland. Kits `999763` and `478604` carry downstream
FTE32242 and converge one generation lower at James M. `Glasgow-193`, through
different sons. If those descendant pedigrees are correct and `200475` is
genuinely negative for FTE32242, the SNP marks the James-193 branch rather than
Robert-12 generally. A Big Y upgrade of
Kilwaughter kit `254947` or `B835762`, followed by private-variant comparison,
is the fastest way to learn which tested line is closest to the documented
Kilwaughter men.

## 24 August 2026: Lisnagaver collateral and land-succession evidence

The 1846 civil marriage of John Glasgow of Lisnagaver supplies a new indirect
route to Alexander's parentage. John was a full-age bachelor and farmer of
Lisnagaver, and his father was explicitly recorded as **John Glasgow,
farmer**. He married Margaret Garvin on 16 November 1846. The certificate is
saved as `sources/john_glasgow_margaret_garvin_marriage_1846.pdf`.

The surviving 1851 census then places three Glasgow households together at
Lisnagaver:

* house 34: John Glasgow, farmer, with Samuel Glasgow explicitly described as
  his brother;
* house 35: Alexander Glasgow, farmer, with Mary and their children;
* house 37: Thomas Glasgow, farmer.

John and Samuel are therefore proved brothers and their father was named John
on the ordinary reading of the marriage record. The census does **not** call
Alexander or Thomas their brothers, so proximity alone cannot extend that
parentage to them.

The land evidence nevertheless makes a relationship worth testing. In the
1834 tithe book Alexander Glasgow held 29 acres and Robert Glasgow held 35
acres at Lisnagaver. In the first valuation, John Glasgow's lot 24 contained
21 acres 2 roods 25 perches and Alexander Glasgow's adjoining lot 25 contained
8 acres 2 roods 10 perches. Together those holdings closely reconstruct the
earlier 29-acre Alexander holding. Thomas Glasgow's lot 26a contained 32 acres
3 roods 15 perches, closely matching Robert's earlier 35-acre holding.

A Registry of Deeds mortgage dated 8 May 1886 confirms that the Thomas branch's
farm remained a distinct block. Mary Ann Glasgow, widow, and Daniel Glasgow,
farmer, both of Lisnagaver, mortgaged a farm of 33 acres 3 roods 36 perches;
Mary Ann was stated to hold it as tenant from year to year. The deed does not
state their relationship, but its acreage materially strengthens the proposed
Robert-to-Thomas farm continuity. An 1881 judgment memorial separately says
that Thomas Glasgow of Lisnagaver, farmer, was then deceased; it supplies no
parentage.

### Descendant-post reassessment

The 2020 replies to the 2007 Genes Reunited post add Griffith's Valuation observations but no source for the claimed parents Alexander and Rosanna or Rosanna's reported death on 23 October 1822. The post remains a useful descendant tradition, not evidence establishing the parents.

### Current parentage assessment

Two competing models remain:

1. **Older Alexander and Rosanna:** supported only by the unsourced descendant tradition concerning Thomas; compatible with the name Alexander and the 1834 Alexander Glasgow tenancy, but not independently proved.
2. **A father named John:** John Glasgow's 1846 marriage directly names his father as John Glasgow, farmer. John and Samuel are proved brothers, and John was Alexander's immediate neighbour. This model applies to Alexander only if an unproved sibling relationship between Alexander and John can be established.

The land pattern remains suggestive rather than dispositive. The decisive outstanding source remains the early Kilraughts Reformed Presbyterian register and society lists. No new record located in this pass names Alexander's parents.
## 24 August 2026: early marriage, baptisms and Gortereagh residence

### Marriage year established by the 1851 census

Alexander and Mary each reported that they married in **1836**. This is not merely a later tree estimate: it appears in the marriage-year field of their 1851 Lisnagaver census household.

Rasharkin Presbyterian's surviving marriage register begins in 1845, so it cannot supply their 1836 marriage. The reported year nevertheless narrows any search in another congregation or presbytery record.

### Rasharkin Presbyterian baptisms

The published transcription of the original Rasharkin Presbyterian register, PRONI `MIC1P/292`, identifies the family before the 1851 census:

* entry 75: **James Glascaw**, born 17 January 1837 and baptised 9 February 1837, son of Alex and Mary McAughin, residence Gortereagh;
* entry 106, in the 1838 sequence: an unnamed or illegible **Glascow** child of Alex and Molly McAughern, residence Gortereagh; the baptism date, birth date and child's forename were not transcribed;
* the register transcription has no entries for 1840 or 1841, the probable period of son Robert's birth;
* entry 203: **James Glascow**, born 9 October 1843 and baptised 7 January 1844, son of Alex and Molly McAughern, residence Lisnagaver.

The 1837 James must have died before the name was reused for the child born in 1843. No burial or death record for the first James has been located. The illegible 1838 entry is potentially important because its child's forename might preserve a grandparent's name; the original image should be inspected rather than guessed from the transcription.

### Residence sequence and the 1834 Alexander

The evidence now supports this sequence:

* 1834: an Alexander Glasgow held 29 acres at Lisnagaver in the tithe applotment;
* 1836: Alexander and Mary married;
* 1837-1838: their children were baptised from Gortereagh;

Alexander-951 was about 23 in 1834 and was old enough to be the tithe occupier before his marriage. There is therefore no need to posit an older same-named father to explain the 1834 entry. In the absence of a distinguishing designation, the tithe entry is at least as plausibly Alexander-951 as `Glasgow-3904`.

This materially weakens the current older-Alexander parent hypothesis. Apart from the unsourced descendant post, no independent record yet establishes that a separate older Alexander existed in this family.

### Gortereagh and the McCaheran family

No Glasgow occupier appears in the 1834 Gortereagh tithe list. It does include **William and James McCaheran**, a local spelling compatible with McAughin/McAughern/McCaughan. Alexander and Mary's residence there immediately after marriage may therefore reflect Mary's family or a subtenancy rather than a Glasgow holding.

The first known son was named James, and a James McCaheran was at Gortereagh in 1834. This is a useful maternal-family hypothesis, not proof that James was Mary's father. The name could commemorate either side of the family, and the illegible 1838 child's name remains unknown.

The 1851 Lisnagaver census also contains a substantial McCaheren cluster, including widow Sarah McCaghrin, aged 70, and several younger households. None is expressly related to Mary Glasgow, so no parent should be assigned from proximity alone.

### Grave-inscription search

The Ulster Historical Foundation gravestone index returned no Glasgow inscription for Kilraughts. A county-wide Antrim search returned only a Glasgow/Shuter inscription at Inver Church of Ireland. This is limited negative evidence because the index does not cover every grave or graveyard and many families had no surviving stone.

### Revised parentage assessment

* **Older Alexander and Rosanna:** now weaker. The descendant tradition remains the only evidence, and the 1834 tithe Alexander may be Alexander-951 himself.
* **Father John:** still possible only through the unproved proposition that Alexander was brother of John and Samuel. John's 1846 marriage directly names his own father as John, but no source extends that statement to Alexander.
* **Another father, possibly James:** the first son's name and the lack of a proved older Alexander make this possible, but naming custom is not adequate evidence.

No parent can yet be assigned confidently. The highest-value unresolved records are the original 1838 baptism entry, the early Kilraughts membership/death material (especially the reported Rosanna death), and the 1790 Lisnagaver rental.
