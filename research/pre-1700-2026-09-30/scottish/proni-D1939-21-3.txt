'A paper relating to the estate of Lifford [1678-c.1697].
In the year 1678 or thereabouts, Hugh Hamill purchased the lordship of Lifford with other lands in the county of Donegal from Richard Hansard Esq. for a valuable consideration [£3,452], and the said Hugh Hamill not having ready money to pay for the said purchase did enter into a statute staple with Abraham Creighton of Crum Esq. and John Johnson of Clare in the county of Tyrone Esq., sureties for the said purchase money to the aforesaid Hansard, which sum amounted to £2,100 or £2,000 for payment of £1,050 at the end of six months after the first day of May or November that shall next fall after the death of the said Hansard's mother.
Abraham Creighton at the same time, with John Johnson aforesaid, became bound for the said Hugh Hamill to the aforesaid Richard Hansard, as I remember, in another statute staple of £2,000 for the payment of £1,000 at a certain day to be paid, which not done, the aforesaid Richard Hansard called to the said Hugh Hamill for his £1,000 then due, upon which the said Hugh Hamill borrowed from Dr John Madden £1,000, and for his security did mortgage unto the said Dr John Madden the lordship of Lifford until the said £1,000 was paid with the arrears thereof, and gave the said Abraham Creighton bound for security; that some time after, the said Hugh Hamill sold a part of this lordship which he had formerly mortgaged to Thomas Glascoe Esq. for £3,216, and for the performance of covenants to the said Glascoe Abraham Creighton aforesaid and John Hamilton Esqs. became bound to the said Glascoe, the said Hamill obliging himself to pay the money received from Glascoe to Dr Madden and others, to whom the said Creighton stood bound for the said Hamill, but ... received the £3,216 and paid it to other creditors and left the said Creighton in the lurch for at least £10,000 or £12,000, which may appear by bonds and judgment now upon record.
Hugh Hamill, his case being so despicable, Dr Maddein commenced suit against Abraham Creighton for his money and interest, which came then to about £1,450; so that the said Creighton was necessitated to purchase the said mortgage from Dr Maddin and pay him down in hand £845 and secure him the remainder of his money; that Mr Thomas Glascoe commenced a suit against the said Abraham Creighton for £5,000 and some odd hundreds of pounds, his estate being seized by elegits for debts owing to Mr Alwith and Mr Savage and damnified, as he alleges, £700, so that the said Creighton was necessitated to sell and encumber a great part of his own free estate, by which he purchased Glascoe's, which Mr Savage delivered the possession then in him to the said Creighton; and ever since the said Creighton has been possessed of the mortgage and the concerns of Hugh Hamill he hath been paying off debts wherein he stood bound for the said Hamill, as may appear.' (c.1700).
'Articles of agreement made and concluded upon by and between the Hon. Major-General David Creighton and Abraham Creighton Esq., his son, of the one part, and Col. Alexander Montgomery of the other part, in relation to the borough of Lifford in the county of Donegal.
Imprimis - It is hereby mutually agreed between the said parties that their respective interests in the said borough or corporation of Lifford shall be during their respective lives united, and that the said Major-General Creighton and Col. Alexander Montgomery shall be from time to time, as often as there is occasion, elected members of parliament for the borough or corporation, but in case any of the present members of parliament shall die during the continuance of this present parliament, that then and in such case, the said Abraham Creighton shall be elected member of parliament in the room or place of such of the said members as shall happen first to die.
Secondly - It is likewise agreed that in case the said Alexander Montgomery shall in any succeeding parliament during the life of the said Major-General Creighton be elected a member of parliament for any other borough or corporation of this kingdom, that then the said Abraham Creighton shall be elected member of parliament for the said borough or corporation of Lifford.
Thirdly - It is also agreed by the said parties that in case the said Alexander Montgomery and Abraham Creighton shall survive the said Major-General Creighton, that then the said Alexander Montgomery and Abraham Creighton shall have an equal power in the said borough, and that they two or such person as either of them shall recommend shall be elected member of parliament for the said borough, it being the intent of the said parties that the said Alexander Montgomery and Abraham Creighton, after the death of the said Major-General Creighton, shall have an equal interest in choosing or electing members of parliament for the said borough.
Fourthly - It is likewise agreed that in case the said Alexander Montgomery shall have a son who shall survive him, that such son after the death of the said Alexander Montgomery, as soon as he shall be qualified to serve as member of parliament, shall from time to time during his life be elected a member of parliament for the said borough, provided the said Major-General Creighton during his life, or the said Abraham Creighton after his decease, shall have interest so to do, and provided such son shall agree to all the articles in these presents contained in relation to the management of the said borough in the same manner that the said Col. Montgomery has agreed to perform.
Fifthly - It is likewise agreed by the within parties that no warden or burgess shall be elected any time hereafter but by and with the consent of the said Major-General Creighton and Col. Alexander Montgomery during the life of the said Major-General Creighton, and after his decease but by and with the consent of the said Alexander Montgomery and Abraham Creighton, in case they survive the said Major-General.
Sixthly - It is also agreed that in case the said Alexander Montgomery shall survive both the said Major-General Creighton and Abraham Creighton, that then the said Alexander Montgomery shall join with such person who shall have the inheritance of the lands of the said borough in the same manner as it is agreed between the said Alexander Montgomery and Abraham Creighton in the third article within-mentioned in relation to the management of the said borough, provided such person or persons, who shall have the inheritance of the lands of said borough, be heir or heirs of the said Major-General Creighton, and that such heir of heirs shall concur or agree to all the articles contained in these presents; and for the performance of all these agreements and articles all the said parties do hereby solemnly declare upon their honours that they will perform and fulfil as much as in their power every article and agreement in these presents contained, and have hereunto signed their names in presence of witnesses hereto subscribing, this 16th day of June anno domini 1727.
Witness present; Alexander Nesbitt, Abraham Hamilton, David Creighton, Abraham Creighton Alexander Montgomery. (16 June 1727).
Letter from Col. Alexander Montgomery, Arbor Hill, Dublin, to Abraham Creichton assuring him of the quiet possession of the Corporation of Lifford, if the writer dies without heirs of his own.
"Sir, I do heartily condole with you for the loss of so good a friend, whom I believe all that knew as well as I did do sincerely lament.
I can by no means think Mr William Creighton a proper burgess for Lifford (though he be a young man for whom I have a great regard); for his distant situation from the place, and care he has and in all likelihood will always have by the function he is of, will render him entirely useless. You know there has been great want of members near the place to discharge the necessary trust annexed to the borough, and therefore I think some person should be chose who resides near the place and who can attend upon occasions.
I hope when you consider that you have already five secure voices in Lifford, and I but four that I can any way depend on, that 'tis but reasonable the person now chose should give me some assurance of his preserving my interest jointly with your's, before I agree to his being admitted. I think in all likelihood my life can't be very long, and I hope, since I have no heirs of my own to succeed me, who are the only persons by my agreement that I have preserved any right for after me, it won't be thought unreasonable I should be secured the choice of one member in that borough, after all the pains and expense I have been at. If you will honour me so far as to let me name the person, I give you my honour I will name such a one as will never betray you nor your's, and one who shall give you all the satisfaction you desire that he will do so. I hope after I have with great trouble and expense preserved you an interest in the town, which but for me you would have lost forever, and after the pains I took (and enemies I made myself on that account) to bring you into the House, it will be in no one's power to make you distrust me. I do assure you I will always preserve your interest in Lifford with the same care I do my own, and will, if I die without a son, leave you in full possession of it, unless ill-usage force me to the contrary; which is what I hope and believe I shall never meet with from the son of Major-General Creighton, with whom I lived in perfect friendship ever since I was capable of any.
You have my best wishes for your success in everything that relates to you, and if I can serve you I owe it to Major-General Creighton and will willingly do it. Give my service to your poor mother and sisters.
I am, Sir, your obedient, humble servant Alexander Montgomery.
I shall be glad you'll let me hear from you or know when I shall wait on you." (6 June 1728).
Letter from Alexander Montgomery, Dublin, to Abraham Creighton: 'Sir, my servant just gave me your's this minute or I would have answered it sooner.
I have already signified my inclinations to the warden and burgesses of Lifford to have you chose, and I believe it will be soon, without you signify your choice of another. If your business will not permit your going down, I would have you name someone of your friends whom you please, to whom I readily agree; but it being your turn I will name no one, being resolved strictly to fulfil all agreements made with you and your father.
I am, Sir, your obedient, humble servant Alexander Montgomery.' (18 June 1728).
