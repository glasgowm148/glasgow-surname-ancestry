== Introduction ==

The '''Glasgow Name Study''' traces the Glasgow surname and its historical variants through records, reconstructed families and Y-DNA. Its story begins with medieval people described as ''de Glasgu''—“of” or “from Glasgow”—and continues through the households of Lowland Scotland, settlement in Ulster and the development of families around the world.

The earliest records reveal merchants, churchmen, royal officials and property witnesses. Sixteenth-century documents bring the story closer: husbands and wives transferring tenements, children succeeding to property, fishers entering the burgess roll, and relatives administering estates. These are the foundations from which the later family branches can be reconstructed.

== 1. From a place-name to a surname, c.1175–1506 ==

=== The earliest bearers ===

The earliest secure direct bearer currently identified by the study is '''Roger de Glasgu''', a witness to a Glasgow burgh and Clyde fishery charter assigned to approximately '''1175–1199'''. The surviving evidence soon places other bearers well beyond Glasgow itself.<ref name="MedievalRegister">Glasgow Name Study, [https://www.wikitree.com/wiki/Space:Bearers_of_the_%27%27de_Glasgu%27%27_Name_and_the_Emergence_of_the_Glasgow_Surname%2C_c.1175%E2%80%931500 ''Bearers of the de Glasgu Name and the Emergence of the Glasgow Surname, c.1175–1500'']; associated [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/early-bearers.html documentary register], consulted 6 September 2026. The individual bearer pages distinguish the underlying editions, assigned dates, disputed readings and identity assessments.</ref>

In Dublin, '''Richard de Glasgu''' and '''Robert de Glasgu''' were admitted consecutively to the guild merchant, while '''Gilbert de Glascu''' appears later in the same roll. Their presence introduces a commercial dimension to the surname's earliest history: the name was already being used to identify people within networks connecting Scotland and Ireland. The adjacent entries invite investigation of a family or trading association, although the roll does not state one.

A Dublin admission in '''1238/39''' identifies '''Alexander as the son of Robert de Glascu'''. This is an early family-identifying use of the name, but an important grammatical distinction remains: the Glasgow designation belongs to the father in the surviving entry, rather than being independently applied to Alexander.<ref name="MedievalRegister" />

Other early occurrences belong to different settings. A Robert de Glascu appears in an English royal plea-roll notice in 1200; a Robert de Glasgu was a clerk in the household of the bishop of Dunkeld; and a Robert de Glascu witnessed a Durham episcopal act. They are retained as separate working identities unless records connect them. Their different settings offer several routes for investigating how the name travelled.

=== Churchmen, officials and local witnesses ===

'''[[Space:John de Glasgu|John de Glasgu]]''' was chaplain to Bishop Gamelin of St Andrews on '''2 February 1259'''. He probably also appears in a Cambuskenneth Abbey charter concerning Dunipace: the recurrence of Richard de Stickley in the witness groups supports that identification.<ref name="John1259">''Registrum Prioratus Sancti Andree'' (Bannatyne Club, 1841), p. 173, act dated 2 February 1259; see [[Space:John de Glasgu]] for the comparison with the Dunipace witness group.</ref>

Other bearers illustrate the breadth of these institutional connections: Master Walter de Glasgu in Melrose charter material, a John of Glasgow in the Holyrood community in 1299, and Richard of Glasgow, a monk of Arbroath, in 1312. These are named people within institutions, not simply references to the bishopric or city of Glasgow.<ref name="MedievalRegister" />

Within Glasgow itself, '''Hugh de Glasgw''' witnessed a Rottenrow property transaction around '''1283'''. The deed already places the transaction in Glasgow, yet Hugh is still individually distinguished by the name. Here ''de Glasgw'' was doing the work of a personal identifier, rather than merely indicating where the deed was made.

'''Alexander de Glasgu or de Glasgow''', royal escheator south of the Forth in '''1289/90''', supplies another strand: a bearer exercising royal administrative responsibilities rather than appearing solely in ecclesiastical records.<ref name="MedievalRegister" />

=== A persistent personal name ===

Repeated usage becomes clearer in the fourteenth century. '''William de Glascou''', vicar of Corbridge, appears in records of '''1315 and 1322'''. '''Master John de Glasgow''' occurs repeatedly in the later fourteenth century, including records naming him as chancellor of Dunkeld; the wider reconstruction also associates him with Aberdeen. His personal designation remains distinguishable from the offices he held.

An Exchequer account of '''1394''' names '''Malcolm, son of John de Glasgu'''. Royal accounts then record '''William, physician de Glasgu''', in '''1395 and 1397'''. These join the merchants, clerics, officials, burgesses and landholders already visible in the record.<ref name="MedievalRegister" />

The cumulative result is more substantial than a collection of incidental place references. Glasgow-derived second names were used repeatedly to identify individuals, sometimes across years and institutions. However, a stable personal byname and an inherited family surname are different stages of the story. Neither the presence nor the absence of ''de'' alone proves hereditary transmission, and ''de Glasgu'' is not evidence of noble status.

The Glasgow burgess and the Stirling witness could have been one mobile man, successive generations, collateral relatives or members of different surname families. Their chronology allows several possibilities; no shared property or explicit relationship presently selects one.

The important development is the continued appearance of recognisable surname forms before the sixteenth-century family records begin. What is missing is not every trace of the name, but the relationships needed to turn those traces into a continuous pedigree.

<span id="burgh_families"></span>
== 2. The first reconstructable Glasgow burgh families ==

The town-clerk protocols change the character of the evidence. Instead of an isolated name in a witness list, they record people acting as parents, spouses, children, heirs and curators. Property boundaries and repeated associates allow one transaction to be compared with another.

A '''sasine''' records possession of property; a '''liferent''' reserves its use or income during someone's lifetime. A '''curator''' assisted a person who required legal support, including a minor. These details matter because inheritance, consent and administration often reveal relationships that parish registers do not preserve.

=== William Glasgow and Esbell Brade ===

'''[[Glasgow-1098|William Glasgow]]''' enters the secure burgh record as a witness on '''28 February 1530/31'''. He appeared in a property transaction on Our Lady's Street alongside other participants in the burgh's legal business.<ref name="William1531">Robert Renwick, ed., ''Abstracts of Protocols of the Town Clerks of Glasgow'', vol. IV, protocol 1058, 28 February 1530/31; [https://archive.org/stream/abstractsprotoc08robegoog/abstractsprotoc08robegoog_djvu.txt digitised text].</ref>

A William Glasgow had also appeared in a royal remission of '''2 July 1526''' concerning the slaughter of James Somervile and other offences. That is a plausible earlier occurrence, but the entry lacks the household and property identifiers required to equate the men securely. The register reference is '''no. 3412'''.<ref name="William1526">M. Livingstone, ed., ''Registrum Secreti Sigilli Regum Scotorum'', vol. I, A.D. 1488–1529 (Edinburgh, 1908), no. 3412, 2 July 1526; [https://archive.org/stream/registrumsecret00scotgoog/registrumsecret00scotgoog_djvu.txt digitised volume and index]. See the [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1098.html William Glasgow case assessment] for the distinction between the remission bearer and the burgh householder.</ref>

The decisive family record is dated '''31 December 1534'''. William, with the consent of '''[[Brade-232|Esbell Brade]]''', transferred a back tenement to '''their son [[Glasgow-1093|John Glasgow]] and his wife [[Spreull-4|Janet or Jonet Spreull]]''', while reserving the profits during the older couple's lives. The property lay on the west side of the route from the Market Cross towards the South Port.<ref name="WilliamJohn1534">Renwick, ''Abstracts of Protocols'', vol. IV, protocol '''1196''', printed p. '''72''', 31 December 1534, 10 a.m.; [https://archive.org/stream/abstractsprotoc08robegoog/abstractsprotoc08robegoog_djvu.txt digitised text], entry headed “Glasgow—Sprewll”. The abstract names Esbell Brade and describes John as “their son”.</ref>

A later inheritance independently supports this relationship. On '''28 October 1564''', John was served heir to his deceased father William. This supplies a firm latest date for William’s death as well as a second explicit father–son record.<ref name="WilliamHeir1564">Renwick, ''Abstracts of Protocols'', vol. III, protocol '''805''', printed p. '''57''', 28 October 1564; [https://archive.org/details/abstractsprotoc06scogoog/page/n69/mode/2up digitised edition]. See also [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1098.html Glasgow-1098 source findings].</ref>

These records establish a family and a property succession, rather than a relationship inferred merely from matching surnames. William's own parents remain the principal missing link between this household and the late-medieval bearers.

The deeds concerning John junior and William name Jonet as the older John's spouse, but do not use the explicit maternal wording found in Stephen's record. That distinction is retained without obscuring the household reconstruction.

=== Stephen, William and the next generation ===

Stephen's life appears through landholding, witnessing, legal responsibility and civic office. He dealt with property in the burgh, participated in sasines and boundary proceedings, and is recorded as a '''water bailie'''. The clerks recorded his participation in burgh affairs; the office attributed to him is water bailie, not town clerk.<ref name="StephenCase">[https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1029.html Stephen Glasgow, Glasgow-1029, catalogue biography and source findings], consulted 6 September 2026. The public-office description is retained with its unresolved precise appointment reference.</ref>

His household is also identifiable. A transaction of '''25 June 1582''' names '''Stephen Glasgow and [[Graham-38221|Margaret Grahame]]''' as spouses.<ref name="StephenMarriage1582">Renwick, ''Abstracts of Protocols'', vol. VIII, protocol '''2431''', printed pp. '''56–58''', 25 June 1582; [https://archive.org/details/abstractsofproto08glas/page/56/mode/2up digitised edition], as identified in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1029.html Stephen's source record].</ref>

A younger '''[[Glasgow-3522|William Glasgow]]''', husband of '''[[Herbison-439|Margaret Herbesoun]]''', was dead by '''15 July 1586''', when '''[[Glasgow-1099|George Glasgow]]''' received sasine as heir to his deceased father William. On '''1 September 1586''', George acted with the consent of his mother Margaret, who held liferent, and of his curators, Sir William Herbesoun and Stephen Glasgow.<ref name="George1586">Renwick, ''Abstracts of Protocols'', vol. IX, protocol '''2903''', printed p. '''103''', 15 July 1586; and protocol '''2916''', printed p. '''107''', 1 September 1586, [https://archive.org/details/abstractsprotoc01scogoog/page/n116/mode/2up digitised edition]. For the identity comparison, see [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-3522.html Glasgow-3522 source findings].</ref>

The father–mother–son group '''William, Margaret and George''' is directly established. Identifying this William with John senior's son of 1565 is a strong reconstruction: chronology, the burgh setting, overlapping witnesses and Stephen's role in George's affairs fit together. Stephen's curatorship is particularly valuable because it places him inside the next generation's legal and property network.

=== Robert Glasgow and the fisher family ===

A second closely associated household centres on '''[[Glasgow-1096|Robert Glasgow]]''', fisher, burgess and property-holder. His wife appears in the protocols as '''Margaret Rowand''', linked on WikiTree under '''[[Rolland-239|Margaret Rolland]]'''. Their property dealings place them beside another John Glasgow, while a December 1573 protocol names Robert and John together as fishers.<ref name="RobertRowand">Renwick, ''Abstracts of Protocols'', vol. VI, protocols '''1903–1904''', 12 February 1572/73; vol. VII, protocol '''2002''', 19 December 1573, and protocol '''2125''', printed p. '''86''', 7 February 1574/75. Source findings and name assessment at [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1096.html Robert Glasgow, Glasgow-1096]. The last record calls Robert and Margaret Rowand spouses.</ref>

The crucial next-generation record is the burgess admission of '''[[Glasgow-3188|Ninian Glasgow]]''' on '''23 October 1596''', which calls him the '''second son of Robert Glasgow, fisher and burgess'''. Occupation, civic standing, place and period make the property-holder a strong identification for this father.<ref name="Ninian1596">James R. Anderson, ed., ''Burgesses & Guild Brethren of Glasgow, 1573–1750'' (Scottish Record Society, 1925), [https://archive.org/details/scottishrecordso43scotuoft/page/24/mode/2up p. 24], entry dated 23 October 1596.</ref>

Ninian's family becomes unusually tangible in two testaments. The testament of '''[[Meik-14|Jonet Meik]]''' names Ninian as her husband and Robert Glasgow as her son. Ninian's own testament, made on '''10 January 1613''', names sons '''Robert and William''' and a brother '''John'''. Taken together, the two records establish Robert as the son of both Ninian and Jonet Meik.<ref name="NinianTestaments">National Records of Scotland, Glasgow Commissary Court, '''CC9/7/5''', images '''82–83''', testament of Jonet Meik; and '''CC9/7/9''', images '''142–145''', testament of Niniane Glasgw, made 10 January 1613 and confirmed 8 May 1613. Relationship readings and transcription limits are set out in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-3188.html Ninian Glasgow's catalogue biography]. The manuscript readings are cited through that transcription, not a new examination of the original images.</ref>

Ninian had remarried by January 1613. The later wife's name is read in the project transcription as '''Jonet Hutchesoun''', with the surname still carrying a palaeographic qualification. William's mother is not established by these documents. Nor does the combination of “second son” and “brother John” establish John's exact birth order: John should not automatically be labelled the eldest son.

This family belonged to the working and property-owning world of the Clyde. Ninian's inventory records household furnishings, vessels, clothing and debts; his bequest of a new gown to his brother is a small but direct glimpse of the people behind the pedigree. His occupation was '''fisher''', not the superficially similar ''flesher'' or butcher.<ref name="NinianTestaments" />

=== Neighbours, spouses and civic associates ===

'''[[Glasgow-1097|Archibald Glasgow]]''' and '''[[Newlands-647|Isobell Newlands]]''' acquired property near the South Port in '''1551''', with a Robert Glasgow among the witnesses. The record establishes the couple and their property; their placement among William and Esbell's children is a separate reconstruction.<ref name="Archibald1551">Renwick, ''Abstracts of Protocols'', vol. I, protocol '''84''', printed p. '''31''', 21 May 1551; [https://archive.org/details/abstractsprotoc07robegoog/page/n59/mode/2up digitised edition]; [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1097.html Archibald Glasgow source assessment].</ref>

The Spreull, Herbesoun, Rowand, Newlands, Grahame, Meik and Hutchesoun connections provide a useful way into this social world. A wife, curator, neighbouring owner or repeated witness can lead to an inheritance record that finally identifies a relationship. These associations are therefore active evidence for investigation, not decorative additions to a family tree.

John and Jonet's marriage establishes a Glasgow–Spreull connection by 1534; it does not settle Jonet's precise place among the Spreulls of Cowden or Ladymuir. The same approach applies to later Cunningham, Stewart, Hamilton, Graham, Scot and Fraser spouses: identify the actual household and its records before extending a connection to a prominent landed family.

== 3. Families beyond the burgh: Ayrshire, Lothian and the Borders ==

The expanding record does not show only one household spreading out from Glasgow. It reveals several local groups whose internal relationships can sometimes be established before their connection to the burgh families is known.

=== Corsinkell and the Ayrshire countryside ===

In the Cunninghame teind material for '''1559–1560''', '''James Glasgow elder and James Glasgow younger''' appear at '''Corsinkell in Stevenston parish'''. The two designations distinguish men at the same holding; the subsequent deed supplies their relationship.<ref name="CorsinkellTeinds">Margaret H. B. Sanderson, “The People of Sixteenth Century Ayrshire”, ''Ayrshire Collections'', vol. XIV, no. 8 (1987), appendix, “The Cunninghame Teind Lists of 1559–60”, printed p. 331, citing Register of Acts and Decreets, vol. 22, fols. 102v–167v; source details in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1168.html James Glasgow's catalogue record].</ref>

On '''9 February 1571''', a reversion concerning Corsinkell described its occupation by James Glasgow elder and '''James and John Glasgow, his sons'''. It directly establishes:

: '''[[Glasgow-1168|James Glasgow elder]]'''
:: → '''[[Glasgow-3921|James Glasgow younger]]'''
:: → '''John Glasgow''', his other named son

The instrument belongs to the Glencairn estate papers. Its witnesses include Porterfields, a Glasgow burgess and a servant of the earl, placing the family within a wider Ayrshire–Glasgow legal network.<ref name="Corsinkell1571">National Records of Scotland, Charter Chest of the Earls of Glencairn, '''GD39/5/59''', [https://catalogue.nrscotland.gov.uk/nrsonlinecatalogue/details.aspx?reference=GD39%2F5%2F59 letters of reversion], 9 February 1571. The official catalogue names the occupiers and witnesses; the document itself is held off site.</ref>

The same network makes a connection with the Glasgow burgh families worth pursuing. It does not yet identify James elder's parents. The Corsinkell group is therefore a documented Ayrshire family with an open upstream connection, not an unrelated family by default and not a proved son-line of William and Esbell.

=== The Calder district and western Lothian ===

'''[[Glasgow-3181|John Glasgow in Hirmanscheillis]]''' died in '''November 1576'''. His testament names his widow '''Margaret Hoghame''' and daughters '''Katherine and Marjorie'''. The place is associated in the project's geographical research with Hirdmanschiels or Hermand in the Calder district.<ref name="Hirmanscheillis1576">National Records of Scotland, Edinburgh Commissary Court, '''CC8/8/5''', image '''276''' and the upper part of image '''277''', testament dative and inventory of Johne Glasgw in Hirmanscheillis. Transcription and place comparison at [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-3181.html Glasgow-3181]. The recorded death is November 1576; an index or confirmation date should not replace it.</ref>

The son John named in the Glasgow protocols of 1563 and 1570/71 is a worthwhile candidate for this man. The outstanding task is to find the bridge between the urban son and the rural testator. By contrast, Ninian's brother John was alive in January 1613 and cannot be the man who died in 1576.

Another household is established by the '''1598 testament of [[Glasgow-3163|Andrew Glasgow]] in Corstoun, Caldercleir'''. It names his spouse '''[[Anderson-103463|Jonet Anderson]]''' and four children: '''Marion, James, Andrew and William'''. The children are stated in the document, whereas Andrew's own proposed father is not.<ref name="Andrew1598">National Records of Scotland, Edinburgh Commissary Court, '''CC8/8/32''', testament of Andro Glasgw, 1598; Scottish Record Society, ''Commissariot of Edinburgh: Register of Testaments, 1514–1600'', p. 108. Transcription and source assessment at [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-3163.html Glasgow-3163].</ref>

Together with '''[[Glasgow-3164|Margaret Glasgow of Croftmalloch]]''', whose testament belongs to 1580, these people define a useful western Lothian research group. Its strength is geographical and documentary: nearby rural households, comparable generations and records within the same commissary jurisdiction. Their exact kinship is still to be established.<ref name="Andrew1598" />

The Caldercleir estate papers offer a particularly promising next step. Rentals and leases may identify a tenant's predecessor, heir or surety, supplying a connection absent from the testament. The relevant Morton papers include '''GD150/696–757''' for Caldercleir and '''GD150/2027–2060''' for Midlothian rentals.<ref name="CaldercleirPapers">National Records of Scotland, GD150, Papers of the Earls of Morton; estate-series references and access assessment recorded in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-3163.html Andrew Glasgow's source findings]. These are research targets, not documents claimed here to have been inspected.</ref>

=== A regional surname network ===

By the early seventeenth century, Glasgow families were established in the burgh and its hinterland, the Stevenston–Kilwinning area, the Calder district, Edinburgh and Leith, and the Borders. Later Lanarkshire testament indexes also reveal a recurring group at '''Quothquan or Quodquen''', rather than an isolated bearer.<ref name="Quothquan">Scottish Record Society, ''The Commissariot Record of Lanark: Register of Testaments, 1595–1800'', p. 38; entries discussed in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-591.html the William Glasgow case file], including James, webster, 1624; John, 1650 and 1662; William, 1673; and John, 1685. An index establishes occurrences, not the relationships between all the testators.</ref>

A plausible historical model is that several connected or partly connected Lowland families developed local branches through work, tenancy, marriage and inheritance. Some may ultimately lead back to one burgh household; others may join only in earlier generations. Keeping the local groups distinct allows those alternatives to be tested rather than decided by the shape of an exported tree.

<span id="religion"></span>
== 4. Religious conflict, ministry and civic life ==

Seventeenth-century religious and political conflict forms part of the family's history, but it affected different households in different ways. The National Covenant of 1638, the struggle over church government and the persecution of nonconformists after the Restoration provide the setting for the directly recorded Covenanter cases.

=== The Cavers prisoners ===

'''[[Glasgow-1498|John Glasgow of Cavers]]''' and a '''William Glasgow''' appear in the Cavers section of the prisoner list associated with the '''1679 wreck of the ''Croune of London'''''. Both are marked as survivors in the account used by the project. The prisoners were being transported after the defeat at Bothwell Bridge.<ref name="Cavers1679">John Henderson Thomson, ed., ''A Cloud of Witnesses'', appendix listing the prisoners transported in 1679, Cavers section and explanation of survivor marks; record assessment in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1498.html John Glasgow of Cavers]. Historical context: Mark Jardine, [https://drmarkjardine.wordpress.com/2016/12/10/beyond-orkneys-fatal-shore-the-wreck-of-the-croune-10-december-1679-history-scotland/ “Beyond Orkney's Fatal Shore: the wreck of the Croune, 10 December 1679”]. The exact edition and appendix page should be supplied with a future direct source-image citation.</ref>

This is a firm connection between named Glasgow bearers and the Covenanter experience. The list does not make John and William brothers, identify their parents or state where either went afterwards. In particular, the listed William has not been securely equated with '''[[Glasgow-1605|the existing William Glasgow profile]]'''.

The Cavers group is nevertheless relevant to the Ulster question. James Young, another man in the same prisoner sequence, is reported among survivors who subsequently reached Ulster. That makes the route a concrete migration hypothesis worth testing for William, rather than a conclusion drawn solely from a later family tradition.<ref name="WilliamTradition">William Melanchthon Glasgow, ''History of the Glasgow Family'', account of the ancestor William and the 1689 campaign; compared with the Cavers prisoner list, Scottish Privy Council references and Ulster-Scots Agency research in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-591.html Glasgow-591 source findings], updated 12 August 2026, consulted 6 September 2026. The family history and the proposed Scottish identification are separate evidence layers.</ref>

=== Kilbirnie, Irvine and the later Ayrshire family ===

'''[[Glasgow-1073|John Glasgow, M.A.]]''' graduated at Glasgow on '''16 July 1674''' and was ordained minister of '''Kilbirnie on 16 August 1688'''. His ministry continued until his death in 1721, spanning the transition to the post-Revolution Presbyterian settlement. His career connects the family directly to the parish ministry of the emerging Presbyterian settlement.<ref name="FastiKilbirnie">Hew Scott, ''Fasti Ecclesiae Scoticanae'', new ed., vol. III (Edinburgh: Oliver and Boyd, 1920), pp. 102–103, especially p. 103; [https://archive.org/details/fastiecclesiaesc03scot digitised volume]. Career and parentage assessment at [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1073.html Glasgow-1073].</ref>

On '''14 January 1690''', he married '''[[Cunningham-20924|Jean Cunningham]]''', daughter of John Cunningham in Wattieston, a chamberlain associated with the Kilbirnie or Glengarnock estate. He later married '''[[Scott-40313|Margaret Scott]]'''.<ref name="MinisterMarriage">Kilbirnie Parish, Old Parish Registers, banns and marriages, entry for John Glasgow and Jeane Cunynghame, '''14 January 1690''', parish '''596''', reference '''10/9''', as cited in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1073.html the captured biography]; James Paterson, ''History of the County of Ayr'', vol. II, pp. 252–253; Scott, ''Fasti'', vol. III, p. 103.</ref>

His family illustrates the movement between education, ministry, civic office and estate service. His son '''[[Glasgow-1077|John Glasgow, M.A.]]''' became a merchant, bailie and provost of '''Irvine'''. Another son, '''[[Glasgow-1075|Dr Robert Glasgow]]''', was a surgeon and chamberlain to Viscount Garnock. The next generation included '''[[Glasgow-1076|Robert Glasgow of Montgreenan]]'''.<ref name="AyrshireFamily">Paterson, ''History of the County of Ayr'', vol. II, pp. 252–253; Ayrshire family and Irvine municipal references collected in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1073.html John Glasgow's catalogue biography]. The Montgreenan account is supplemented below by research on St Vincent plantation ownership.</ref>

The Corsinkell–Stevenston–Kilwinning network provides a promising regional model for the minister’s earlier ancestry. Testing it means following the intervening generations through Ayrshire rentals, testaments and church records; the attached Hugh and John profiles do not yet supply that bridge.<ref name="FastiKilbirnie" />

<span id="ulster"></span>
== 5. Ulster: established households and several migration stories ==

The Irish evidence needs to be read in two different historical settings. The medieval Dublin guild bearers belong to the early history of the name. The seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Ulster households belong to a later period of Scottish–Irish movement. A similar surname on both sides of that gap does not by itself establish an unbroken Irish family.

Within Ulster, the strongest approach is to reconstruct each local household through leases, townlands, congregation records, wills and overseas connections. County-level labels are useful for navigation, but the decisive evidence usually lies at the level of a particular tenancy or family.

=== Larne and Kilwaughter: the earliest local evidence ===

'''[[Glasgow-3970|Robert Glaskow]]''' is described as living in '''Larne''' in an examination taken on '''21 April 1653''', preserved among the 1641 Depositions. The examination recounts violence attributed to the earlier rebellion period and includes hearsay. It is useful evidence for a named Larne man, not a secure account of his parents, occupation or descendants.<ref name="RobertLarne1653">Trinity College Dublin, 1641 Depositions, examination of '''Con O Sheale''', '''21 April 1653''', identifier '''838153r210''', fol. 153r; [https://1641dep.abdn.ac.uk/items/show/42821.html scholarly transcription at the University of Aberdeen]. The passage calls Robert Glaskow a Larne resident; the deponent explicitly reports parts of the account as hearsay. See [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-3970.html the separate Robert Glaskow case file].</ref>

The Larne man has his own research identity, distinct from '''[[Glasgow-1078|the Scottish Robert Glasgow]]'''. Connecting them requires a record that follows the same person across the Irish Sea.

The '''1669 Antrim hearth-money roll''' provides a different kind of evidence. '''[[Glasgow-3913|John or Jo. Glascho]]''' and '''[[Glasgow-1081|James Glascho]]''' appear as separate entries at '''Kilwaughter Demesne'''. Their shared locality, unusual spelling and neighbouring entries make close kinship a reasonable working hypothesis; the roll itself distinguishes two taxable households rather than stating their relationship.<ref name="Kilwaughter1669">PRONI, hearth-money rolls, '''T/307/A''', County Antrim, 1669; Bill Macafee's [https://www.billmacafee.com/1660shearthmoneyrolls/1669hearthmoneyrollsantrim.pdf transcription], Kilwaughter Demesne, entries '''5347–5348'''. Source assessment in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1081.html James Glascho's case file]. The roll is used here through that transcription and assessment.</ref>

Later congregational material places a James Glasgow within Kilwaughter's Presbyterian life. The sequence assembled by the project includes church responsibilities around 1700, session membership in 1721 and service as a congregational commissioner in 1722. It makes a coherent local career to investigate alongside the hearth-roll household, while allowing for the possibility of successive men with the same name.<ref name="KilwaughterCongregation">Classon Porter, congregational memoirs of Larne and Kilwaughter in ''The Christian Unitarian'', vol. II (1863), pp. 409–414, and vol. III (1864), pp. 15–19, 196–198 and 379–380; [https://archive.org/details/christianunitari0002unse vol. II] and [https://archive.org/details/christianunitari0003unse vol. III]. Event and identity assessment at [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1081.html Glasgow-1081].</ref>

=== Robert Glasgow, Rachel Wilson and the South Carolina connection ===

A more firmly reconstructable eighteenth-century household is that of '''[[Glasgow-1143|Robert Glasgow]] and [[Wilson-78088|Rachel Wilson]]''' at Kilwaughter. The congregation-register chronology reports their marriage in November 1729 and the birth of their son '''[[Glasgow-1144|James Glasgow]] on 24 May 1731'''.<ref name="KilwaughterRegister">PRONI, '''MIC/1B/6''', Kilwaughter congregation records, as transcribed in the [https://web.archive.org/web/20101124184247id_/http://lifesweaving.com/Glasgow/Kilwaughter/Timeline.html archived Kilwaughter chronology]. The derivative chronology is assessed alongside independent deed and equity material in [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1143.html Glasgow-1143] and [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1144.html Glasgow-1144].</ref>

An independent lease of '''13 February 1738''' names Robert of Kilwaughter and his wife Rachel, formerly Wilson. It concerns '''63 acres at Ballykeel and 15 acres at Upper Ballyheel''', and also names James Glasgow junior as a life in the lease.<ref name="KilwaughterLease1738">Registry of Deeds, Ireland, '''vol. 105, p. 301, memorial 74015''', deed dated '''13 February 1738''', registered '''12 April 1742'''; [https://www.irishdeedsindex.net/mem.php?memorial=74015 Registry of Deeds Index Project extract]. This is a derivative abstract; the wife's relationship is explicit, while the naming of a lease life alone is not proof of parentage.</ref>

The family's later American records carry the relationships across the Atlantic. James's will, made in '''1775''' and proved in '''1793''', names his wife Mary and six children. In '''1817–1818''', South Carolina equity proceedings concerning the Wilson inheritance identify James as Rachel's son; his son '''[[Glasgow-1983|Robert Glasgow]]''' calls James his father and Rachel his paternal grandmother.<ref name="JamesWillEquity">James Glasgow, will dated '''17 October 1775''', proved '''13 July 1793''', Will Book A, p. 194; equity complaint of 1817, Robert Glasgow's sworn answer of 2 June 1817 and amended answer of 18 June 1818. Concordant transcriptions and their source links are collated at [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1144.html James Glasgow, Glasgow-1144]. The original court and register images remain the final verification layer; the conclusions here are drawn from the identified transcriptions, not from inspection of the complete original files.</ref>

Here, church chronology, an Irish lease and American litigation reinforce one another. A family holding land at Kilwaughter can be followed into South Carolina through named relationships, while the later inheritance dispute reaches back across the Atlantic to recover its Irish kin.

=== Tyrone, Derry and the other working groups ===

A later family history remembers '''[[Glasgow-591|William Glasgow]]''' as a Scot who remained in northern Ireland following the '''1689 campaign'''. It supplies a potentially meaningful family tradition, but not a Scottish baptism, a military unit or an identified founding tenancy. The Cavers candidate provides a route to test; it is not yet the recovered biography of this traditional ancestor.<ref name="WilliamTradition" />

The Tyrone–Derry records should also be approached through their own local families, rather than made subordinate to a supposed universal founder. The working groups below are research entry points, not four proved son-lines:

<span id="worldwide"></span>
== 6. Migration and worldwide families ==

Glasgow families eventually established themselves across North America, the Caribbean, Australia, New Zealand and elsewhere. Their histories include farmers, tradespeople, merchants, soldiers, ministers, professionals and families whose movements are best followed through ordinary civil and church records.

Migration was not simply a one-way sequence of Scotland → Ulster → America. Some families left Scotland directly; others spent generations in Ireland; others moved from Ireland to Scotland. The '''[[Glasgow-951|Alexander Glasgow]]''' line, represented in the DNA project and associated with County Antrim before Scotland, is one of the latter research strands.<ref name="DNAExplorer">Glasgow Surname Project, [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/ydna/ Y-DNA Explorer], consulted '''6 September 2026'''. Topology and estimates are quoted from this project snapshot, not a newly retrieved FamilyTreeDNA date table. Tester pedigrees are evaluated separately from their genetic placements.</ref>

=== Some distinctive overseas histories ===

'''[[Glasgow-634|James Glasgow]]''' became North Carolina's first Secretary of State. His career also included the land-fraud scandal that led to the abandonment of the name Glasgow County in favour of Greene County. His career links the surname to the politics, landholding and public administration of the new American state.<ref name="JamesSecretary">Charles R. Holloman, [https://www.ncpedia.org/biography/glasgow-james “Glasgow, James”], ''Dictionary of North Carolina Biography'', reproduced by NCpedia; see also NCpedia, [https://www.ncpedia.org/geography/greene “Greene County”].</ref>

'''[[Glasgow-1220|William Glasgow, later Glass]]''', a Scottish soldier, remained on '''Tristan da Cunha in 1817''' with his South African wife Maria and their children when the garrison withdrew. Alongside Samuel Burnell and John Nankivel, he helped establish the permanent community. Their agreement of 7 November 1817 provided for shared resources and equal participation. This is a documented example of a Glasgow family continuing under another surname form.<ref name="WilliamGlass">Tristan da Cunha Government and Association website, [https://www.tristandc.com/history1817-1853.php “A Growing Community, 1817–1853”]; British Library, [https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002056989 Add MS 43846], William Glass and Tristan da Cunha papers. The Glasgow-to-Glass identity is discussed on [[Glasgow-1220]].</ref>

'''[[Glasgow-473|Ellen Glasgow]]''' won the '''1942 Pulitzer Prize for the Novel''' for ''In This Our Life''.<ref name="EllenPulitzer">The Pulitzer Prizes, [https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/ellen-glasgow Ellen Glasgow], 1942 winner in the Novel category.</ref> '''[[Glasgow-773|Sir Thomas William Glasgow]]''', born in Queensland, became a soldier, politician and diplomat; his father Samuel was a farmer from northern Ireland.<ref name="ThomasAustralia">Ralph Harry, [https://adb.anu.edu.au/biography/glasgow-sir-thomas-william-6397 “Glasgow, Sir Thomas William (1876–1955)”], ''Australian Dictionary of Biography'', National Centre of Biography, Australian National University.</ref>

=== The Caribbean and plantation wealth ===

The worldwide history also includes slavery and the movement of plantation wealth into Scottish estates. '''[[Glasgow-1076|Robert Glasgow of Montgreenan]]''' owned two plantations on '''St Vincent'''. Research on British country houses connects his West Indian fortune with the acquisition and development of Montgreenan in Ayrshire.<ref name="MontgreenanSlavery">Simon D. Smith, “Slavery's heritage footprint: links between British country houses and St Vincent plantations, 1814–1834”, in Madge Dresser and Andrew Hann, eds., ''Slavery and the British Country House'' (English Heritage, 2013), especially '''p. 62'''; [https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/slavery-and-british-country-house/slavery-british-country-house-web/ digitised volume]. The account identifies Robert Glasgow's two St Vincent plantations and the connection between colonial wealth and Montgreenan.</ref>

This belongs in the family history alongside ministry, public office and migration. It also makes Caribbean research important in its own right: the study should follow the people and households found in the records, rather than assume that every bearer of the surname overseas follows the same biological or legal route into the family.

== 7. Y-DNA: connecting the paternal branches ==

Y-DNA adds a different kind of evidence. Documentary genealogy asks which named person was the father of another; Y-DNA compares paternal lines and helps determine whether they share a male-line ancestor, how their branches divide and approximately when those divisions occurred.

The principal tested Glasgow surname cluster is defined by '''[[Space:R-FT25406|R-FT25406]]'''. The study also retains separately tracked genetic groups: membership of the surname study does not require belonging to this one paternal lineage.

=== Start with the tester's supported ancestor ===

The most productive comparison begins with each tester’s earliest supported ancestor. The explorer retains the '''[[Glasgow-951|Alexander Glasgow]]''', '''[[Glasgow-2738|James J. Glasgow]]''' and reported '''[[Glasgow-591|William Glasgow]]''' roots as distinct documentary problems.<ref name="DNAExplorer" /> The '''[[Glasgow-12|Robert Glasgow, 1749–1839]]''' family provides a further comparison between researched descendant lines.<ref name="Robert1749">[https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-12.html Robert Glasgow, Glasgow-12, biography and source findings], consulted 6 September 2026; see the distinction between the documented descendant family and proposed earlier parentage.</ref>

This allows the research to move in both directions. A genetic match can prioritise a documentary connection between two local families; independently documented descendants of different sons can help place a genetic subdivision within a known pedigree. Neither exercise requires attaching every line to an unproved seventeenth-century founder first.

'''STR similarity and confirmed SNP placement are recorded separately.''' STRs compare repeat-marker patterns and can suggest close paternal neighbourhoods. A named downstream SNP branch requires the relevant mutation result, not simply a close STR match or an administrator's provisional grouping. The Robert-and-Rachel / James-of-Kilwaughter lines are particularly useful candidates for further resolution because their documentary reconstruction can be compared with the existing genetic leads.<ref name="KilwaughterDNA">[https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-1144.html James Glasgow source assessment] and [https://glasgow.phenotype.dev/people/glasgow-591.html William-attributed line assessment], consulted 6 September 2026. The Kilwaughter-associated marker-only results must not be presented as confirmed FT20271 tests.</ref>

| An heirship, shared heritable property, a charter naming a relationship, or a coherent succession across the late-fifteenth/early-sixteenth-century gap.
|-
| '''How did the sixteenth-century households connect?'''
| Reconstruct adjoining tenements and the recurring Spreull, Herbesoun, Rowand and Newlands networks; compare the Corsinkell occupiers with the burgh families.
| A testament, marriage contract, reversion, curator appointment or property succession stating the missing relationship.
|-
| '''Which burgh families entered Ayrshire or the Calder district?'''
| Follow local tenancy successions, especially the Corsinkell/Glencairn and Corstoun/Caldercleir material, and the intervening Ayrshire minister-family generations.
| A predecessor or heir named at the same holding, a spouse's testament, or a record giving both the old and new residence.
|-
| '''Which Scots founded particular Ulster households?'''
| Keep the Larne man, the Kilwaughter hearth-roll households, the Tyrone tradition and the Moneymore–Cookstown families distinct while comparing associates and estate records.
| A lease, military or church entry, probate document, letter or deed that identifies the same person on both sides of the Irish Sea.
|-
| '''Where do the tested paternal branches meet?'''
| Extend independently sourced tester pedigrees and resolve promising marker-only lines through appropriate additional genetic evidence.
| Matching documentary male-line chains, or a new genetic subdivision that meaningfully narrows which local families should be compared.
|}

Naming patterns and repeated marriages can make one hypothesis more promising than another. Their best use is to generate specific predictions: a missing son should appear in a particular estate succession, a supposed brother in an administration, or an emigrant's relatives among witnesses. A hypothesis becomes stronger when the next independently found record matches those predictions.

== Contributing and maintaining the study ==

Contributions are especially useful when they include the complete record image or transcript, the archive reference and page or folio, and an explanation of the identity or relationship it supports. A record that distinguishes two same-named people can be as valuable as one that connects a new generation.

Researchers and descendants can join the [https://groups.io/g/glasgowdnaproject Glasgow DNA Project discussion group]. Documentary contributions are welcome from every branch, including families outside the principal Y-DNA cluster.

=== Project standards ===

The study records '''occurrence, identity, relationship and lineage''' separately. A source may prove that a John Glasgow existed, while leaving uncertain which John he was and who his parents were.

{| class="wikitable" style="width:100%;"
! Area
! Practice
|-
| '''Sources'''
| Prefer original images and official archive descriptions. Identify published abstracts, indexes, modern transcriptions and family histories as such; never imply that a manuscript was inspected when only a derivative account was available.
|-
| '''Relationships'''
| Use explicit relationship records or a transparent argument from converging evidence. Preserve a promising reconstruction as a labelled hypothesis rather than converting it into a proved connection.
|-
| '''Dates and names'''
| Separate recorded dates from estimates. Distinguish a will's execution, the person's death and probate or confirmation. Retain original spellings in quotations while linking recognisable modern profile names.
|-
| '''Corrections'''
| Explain why an identification or relationship changes. Preserve the source trail and useful rejected hypotheses; avoid speculative merges and blanket removal of unresolved information.
|-
| '''Pre-1500 people'''
| Maintain an individual free-space page for each distinct bearer and link it from the chronological register. Do not create a speculative person profile to complete a pedigree.
|-
| '''DNA and privacy'''
| Distinguish confirmed variants, provisional marker groupings and reported pedigrees. Document the male-line bridge used to infer an ancestral placement; share living testers' personal information only with permission.
|-
| '''Catalogue and maps'''
| Treat exported trees, coordinates and automated comparisons as research aids. Cite the record and identity argument that justify a conclusion, not the score or display alone.
|}
