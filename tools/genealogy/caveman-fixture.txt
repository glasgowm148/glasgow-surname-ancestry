In the spring of 1812, the parish clerk of Dunross recorded a sequence of ordinary
events concerning farms, marriages, and burials along the river. The surviving
register is a later copy, and its heading identifies the parish but does not name
the clerk who made the copy. Entries are arranged by date, although several pages
were recopied after damp damaged the original leaves. A date written in the margin
therefore belongs to the entry only when the page layout and the surrounding
entries agree.

On 14 April, Alexander Fraser and Janet McLean were married after the banns had
been called on three successive Sundays. The entry gives their names, the parish,
and the date of the marriage. It does not give their ages, parents, occupations,
or residences beyond the parish heading. A later note says that the witnesses were
Donald Fraser and Mary Ross, but the note is written in a different hand and is not
explicitly attached to the marriage line. The record supports the marriage date
and place; it does not by itself establish either party's ancestry or identify the
witnesses as relatives.

In June, the clerk recorded repairs to the stone bridge and a payment to a mason.
The account names the bridge but gives no map reference, and the amount is written
with an abbreviation used elsewhere in the book. A second account, dated in July,
lists grain delivered to the poorhouse. The two accounts show local administration
but contain no statement linking the Fraser or McLean families to the bridge or to
the poorhouse. The same surname appearing in an account and a family register is
not proof that the named people are the same individuals.

The burial section contains an entry for a child called James, son of William
Fraser, on 3 October. The entry gives no mother's name and no age. A separate line
mentions a James Fraser in connection with a tenancy, but its date is unclear and
the handwriting differs. These entries should remain separate until an independent
record supplies an age, residence, occupation, or other identifying detail. The
register can support the burial event and the stated father, subject to the normal
limits of a copied parish source; it cannot safely supply missing relationships.

At the end of the year, the clerk added an index of surnames. The index is useful
for locating pages but compresses several entries into one line and sometimes
omits given names. An index reference is therefore a finding aid rather than a
replacement for the dated register entry. Any conclusion drawn from it should be
checked against the page image, with uncertain readings marked as uncertain and
with later family traditions kept distinct from the original record.
