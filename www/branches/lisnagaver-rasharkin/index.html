The question at the heart of this study

Which earlier family links are supported by the combined records, and how strong are they?

John Glasgow, farmer, named in John Glasgow’s 1846 marriage, is assessed 98% for that direct father relationship. Samuel’s father is confidently inferred at 95% from the explicit1851 brother relationship. Alexander’s father is confidently inferred at 90% from Nancy’s probate family network and the recorded Glasgow birth surnames. These strengthen the latter two earlier probable assessments without inventing a named parent statement. The 1796 Desertoghill identity and any Larne ancestry remain unproved; the Thomas–Daniel family is still separate. Jane Dysart’s inherited older-Alexander father is replaced locally by the same record-defined John, at 92% confident inference, from the explicit niece, baptism, will and family witness chain; Rosanna’s maternity remains separate.

What this does not show

The 90% and 95% father inferences do not prove the older John’s birthplace, the 1796 flax-grower identity, a mother or the Larne ancestry. Nancy’s probate citation is incomplete; the underlying petition remains uninspected.

The 1892 probate citation coherently identifies Jane Dysart’s wider child group. Mary Anne’s appearance as a witness at Ann Glasgow’s 1875 marriage independently links the Dysart descendants to John John Glasgow’s household. That John’s 1846 marriage names father John Glasgow, farmer. The combined kinship, recorded Glasgow birth surnames, compatible generations and matched households support the same record-defined father for Jane at 92% confident inference, replacing the unsupported older-Alexander pedigree.

No inspected record directly names Jane’s father. The citation is incomplete and is not an adjudicated pedigree; the underlying petition and heir schedule remain uninspected. Glenleslie: John becomes a possible working father, 38%

The original 1851 Glenleslie household 21 return records John Glasgow as 100, Rose as 80, and 1791 for both. Its marriage heading asks “In what Year Married, or in what Years if more than once?” The detailed transcription’s first-marriage field is therefore an editorial description, not the literal question on this return. Only 1791 is entered: an earlier union is not documented, and the request for multiple years means its absence still weighs against a straightforward earlier-marriage explanation. The original also calls the forty-year-old John a nephew and gives his occupation as vagrant; the 1839 marriage belongs to him. The mortality table explicitly calls Andrew Glasgow, a thirty-year-old linen weaver who died of consumption in winter 1848, a son of the head. It does not name Andrew’s mother.

Select John John Glasgow as the possible working father of the Glenleslie Robert represented by Robert Glasco, at 38% subjective confidence. The original makes their reported age difference 28 years (100 versus 72), a comfortable father–son interval. They were Antrim-born Glasgow farmers in Glenleslie households 21 and 23, separated by the Hamill household. This is a specific nearby older family, and John is the best identified local father comparison already retained in the research. Reading the original marriage question removes the previous overstatement that it expressly records a first marriage in 1791. The combined generation and neighbouring-family argument is sufficient to select a weak working model rather than leave this preferred named comparison outside the tree; it is not a parent statement.

38% assesses the complete John-to-Robert profile hypothesis, not the certainty of reading the census. Robert’s census-implied birth about 1778–1779 precedes the sole reported marriage year by roughly twelve years. An earlier union, nonmarital fatherhood or a reporting error would still be needed, and none is established. The original asks for multiple marriage years, so omission of an earlier year remains a real limitation. John’s exceptional age may be inaccurate. An uncle, cousin, other collateral relative or unrepresented father remains possible; the older Alexander comparison is not excluded. Rose’s reported age does not fit her as Robert’s mother, and no mother or earlier wife is assigned. The selection concerns the 1851 Glenleslie man only: it does not identify him with the Lisnagaver tithe occupier, validate every attached child, or prove a Y-DNA pedigree. Hugh Glasgow’s 74% brother hypothesis includes a separate identity uncertainty and is not automatically assigned this father; his estimated earlier generation also needs its own chronology test. Households and possible groups

Each card is either a household, people whom a record documents together, or a possible group, people studied together because they appear in the same records or place. People in a possible group are not proved to be related; the label on each card gives the strength of the evidence.
